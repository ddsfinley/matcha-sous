Usucha vs Koicha: The Two Styles of Matcha Explained

Two preparations, one leaf: what separates thin tea from thick tea, when to make each, and how to get both right at home.

Matcha Sous Journal · Usucha vs koicha

If you've spent any time exploring matcha, you've probably come across two Japanese terms that sound technical but describe something simple: how thick the tea is. The usucha vs koicha distinction is the most fundamental concept in matcha preparation, and understanding it changes how you think about every cup. One is the bright, frothy bowl most of us know. The other is a rare, intense, almost syrupy experience reserved for the finest leaf.

Here's what separates them, how each is prepared, and which one is right for you.

What Is Usucha (Thin Tea)?

Usucha (薄茶) literally means "thin tea." It's the everyday style of matcha, the one served in most tea rooms and the basis for nearly every matcha latte and café drink. It uses a relatively small amount of powder whisked into a larger volume of water, producing a light, frothy bowl with a delicate foam on top.

Usucha is brisk and refreshing. Because it's more diluted, it carries a touch more natural astringency, balanced by sweetness in a good-quality matcha. It's approachable, quick to make, and the right starting point for anyone new to whisking.

Typical usucha ratio:

  • 2 g matcha (about 1 teaspoon, sifted)
  • 60–70 ml water at 80°C / 175°F
  • Whisked briskly for 15–30 seconds until foamy

What Is Koicha (Thick Tea)?

Koicha (濃茶) means "thick tea," and it's a different experience entirely. It uses roughly double or triple the matcha with much less water, creating a smooth, glossy, paint-like consistency that's kneaded rather than whisked into foam. There's no froth; instead, the surface is even and lustrous.

Koicha is the most prestigious way to drink matcha and the centerpiece of formal tea ceremony. Its flavor is deep, rich, and intensely umami, with almost no astringency. Because there's nowhere to hide, koicha demands the highest grade of matcha, typically the most expensive offerings from a producer, made from the youngest, most shaded leaves. A culinary or everyday matcha will taste unpleasantly bitter prepared this way.

Typical koicha ratio:

  • 4 g matcha (about 2 teaspoons, sifted)
  • 30 ml water at 80°C / 175°F
  • Folded and kneaded slowly until smooth and glossy, not foamed

How to Prepare Each Style

Making usucha

  1. Sift 2 g of matcha into a warmed bowl.
  2. Add 60–70 ml of water at 80°C / 175°F.
  3. Whisk quickly in a "W" motion for 15–30 seconds until a fine layer of foam forms.
  4. Drink promptly, before the powder settles.

Making koicha

  1. Sift 4 g of matcha into a warmed bowl — sifting is non-negotiable here, since lumps ruin the texture.
  2. Add about 15 ml of water first and fold gently to form a paste.
  3. Add the remaining 15 ml gradually, folding and pressing rather than whisking briskly.
  4. Continue until the tea is uniform, thick, and glossy, with no foam.

The contrast in technique matters. Usucha rewards speed and aeration; koicha rewards patience and a gentle hand. Getting a perfectly smooth koicha paste by hand takes practice. Which Style Should You Drink?

For daily enjoyment, lattes, and learning the basics, usucha is the answer. It's forgiving, fast, and works with a broad range of quality matcha.

Reach for koicha when you want to taste a premium matcha at its fullest and you have the grade to support it. Think of it as the difference between an everyday pour and a special tasting. Many people drink usucha all week and save koicha for a quiet weekend ritual.

If you're buying matcha specifically for koicha, prioritize the producer's top tier. For usucha and lattes, a solid mid-range ceremonial matcha delivers excellent results without the premium price.

FAQ

Is koicha just stronger usucha?

Not exactly. It's not only more concentrated; it's prepared differently. Koicha is kneaded into a smooth, foam-free paste, while usucha is whisked to create a frothy surface. The technique and the required matcha grade both differ.

Can I use the same matcha for usucha and koicha?

A high-grade ceremonial matcha can do both. But everyday or culinary matcha works only for usucha and lattes; it turns harsh and bitter when concentrated for koicha. When in doubt, match the grade to the style.

Why does koicha have no foam?

Foam comes from whisking air into thin tea. Koicha has too little water and too much powder to aerate, and the goal is a glossy, even surface instead. The slow folding motion keeps it smooth without introducing bubbles.
